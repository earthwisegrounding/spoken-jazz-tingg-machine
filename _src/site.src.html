    01The PremiseBremerton, WA

    A renaissance in a drab navy town

    Two budding jazz musicians share a house not far from the college they attend on scholarship in a drab Navy town outside of Seattle. A creative community is unwittingly ignited — adding color to the landscape & a renaissance despite the gravity of a blue-collar world.

    1Typewriter

    Spring of 2000, almost finals week at Olympic College, and the music department lounge is full of students before Jazz Band rehearsal — horns blowing faintly from the practice rooms down the hall. Around the oval table sits a cross-section of a town: kids fresh out of high school, and guys who used to be in the Navy band, back on the G.I. Bill.

    One of them lives in a 1973 Volkswagen bus, busks the Bainbridge ferry for a hundred and fifty a day, hosts the Wednesday open mic at a dive with crooked floors, and hauls a late-1940s Remington portable everywhere he goes. He is going to spend the next year turning a shipyard town into a scene.

    The book is written entirely in dialogue — built to be heard. Every scene carries a tune. Play it in order and you have a record.

    03The MachineRemington portable, c. 1947
    tingg* noun — the sound the carriage makes when it hits the margin.

    Not a prop and not a metaphor. In this book the typewriter is cast, credited and played: it keeps time behind the poems, it goes on stage at the dive bar, and it gets its own name in the title.

    I'm writing a bunch & doing this crazy art thing using this typewriter I call the tingg* Machine as not only the esthetic of the project but also a central character. Bub — Act I, Scene 5
    Your typewriter seems to be its own character. — That's exactly it. I seem to take on another persona when my fingers are pounding away on that thing. Dr. Hong & Bub — Act II, Scene 5

      06The AuthorBub Pratt
      Bub & the tingg* Machine

      Poet, guitar player, ferry busker, open-mic host, and for one particular year, the guy who lived in a Volkswagen bus and carried a typewriter into every room he entered.

      Bub self-published three hand-made books in the span of the story you're about to hear — cut with scissors and a glue stick, set in the font of a late-forties Remington, sold for five bucks a copy on the Seattle–Bainbridge run. He fronted a band called Spoken Jazz, playing the typewriter, and made a room full of drinkers in a crooked-floored dive go so quiet you could hear a pin drop.
